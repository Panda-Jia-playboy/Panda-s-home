O Sea, O Sea,
My Home

Yantai is a small city by the sea.

Those of us who grew up in Yantai were raised by the sea breeze, toughened by the slap of the waves, and nourished, little by little, by the food that came from the sea. We were born by the sea, went to school by the sea, and grew up by the sea. In the end, our hearts stayed by the sea, too.

I went to Yantai Foreign Language Experimental School, a high school built right by the sea. It gave me more things to dream about and remember than I could ever count. It was a beautiful place, and the people were more beautiful still. My roommates became some of the dearest people in my life, people I could never bear to part with. They were like brothers to me, my own flesh and blood.

When the hazy moon rose, when moonlight spilled across the sky and drifted like blossoms into the big room the twelve of us shared, that was usually our signal for the night’s “activities” to begin. High school in Shandong could feel stifling by day. At night, we had our little “wild parties,” as wild as we could manage, anyway. Sometimes they barely counted as parties at all.

Spicy snack sticks, crunchy instant noodles, Coke … We’d set out all the forbidden goods on the two beds pushed together and gather around them. Out came the deck of cards we’d brought from home a week earlier. We’d pull off whatever bedsheet was handy and hang it from the top bunk to hide the light. That was our room’s little celebration before a school break. If someone let out a yell, the guy next to him would clap a hand over his mouth. Then we’d all drop flat to keep out of the beam of the dorm monitor’s flashlight as she made her rounds. We’d play until the eastern sky began to turn white and another day was on its way. Outside the window, the waves rolled on just the same.

Some of the guys would quit playing and slip off to one side for a little tea party. I still don’t know how we had so much to talk about back then. We’d go on for hours. We’d talk about our homeroom teacher’s class meeting that day, talk about Dalang’s secret crush, talk about the war between Russia and Ukraine, talk about our futures, talk about our dreams … We called it “the big all-nighter.” The price was steep. Sometimes it earned us Mr. Wang Fei’s personal “wake-up service” in class and a dorm ban from the woman we called Big Sis. Then our great leader, Comrade Room Captain, would claim the blame for himself. With a grand wave of his hand, he’d take the whole “matter of earth-shattering importance” on his shoulders.

The things that seemed so enormous back then look to me now like the first light of morning, brief and beautiful. Just remembering them fills me with a warmth I can hardly put into words.

The sea breeze blew over those boys. Back then, we thought our youth might go on forever.

Here, you and I shared our snacks, traded dreams, and talked about life. I remember one time when one of us did something wrong and was in danger of being expelled. None of us in that room slept a wink that night. The next day, we handed the school a handwritten letter covered in signatures, asking them to forgive him for what he’d done.

Another time, one of the guys got turned down by the girl he liked. We put our arms around him and raised a toast with the beer we’d snuck in. There was the teasing and “fighting” over a single chicken leg at the table, and the time one fart stank so badly it woke the whole room from our noon nap … There are so many of these memories, more than I can tell. You could no more drain them away than you could drain the sea.

The seasons went round. We lived through winters when snow filled the sky and springs bright with blossoms. Together we saw summers when everything green came into bloom and autumns when the cold wind rustled. There’s an old Chinese saying, “Time flies like an arrow, and the years fly like a weaver’s shuttle.” There’s also the image of a white colt glimpsed through a crack in the wall. In the blink of an eye, we’d reached the day we became adults, ready for a coming-of-age ceremony that was ours alone. We were all dressed up that day, looking ahead to the future. The sky was so blue. Sunlight slipped through the cracks in the ceremonial gate and fell across our faces. You stood there, full of spirit, carrying yourself with such grace. I hoped the golden sunlight on your faces would shine beyond that moment, when you all looked so handsome, and light the road ahead. I hoped you could face whatever obstacles lay in your way and whatever coldness you met in the world. What a fine thing it was to be young and full of spirit!

There’s a line from an old Chinese poem that I love. “Ah, generations have come and passed away; From year to year the moons look alike, old and new.” The great river keeps flowing, yet it has been the same since ancient times. The moon brightens and dims, waxes and wanes, as it has for hundreds of millions of years. So it is with the sea. The tide rises and falls, the wind rises and the waves swell, much as they have for ten thousand years. They mean something to us because of the feelings we leave with them and can never forget. Perhaps someone pledged their life to the person they loved on a riverbank. Perhaps someone met a lifelong friend who truly understood them under the moon. Or perhaps, like me, they found by a sea like this a group of people to study with, fool around with, and walk alongside. Life is a journey we walk more and more on our own. The scenery grows more intricate along the way. What’s rare is to have had people beside you who once shared the view with you.

Today, we’re scattered in different places. But when I look back on those days, there’s only one phrase I can think of.

The waves still sound the same.

The lines from Zhang Ruoxu’s poem are quoted in Xu Yuanchong’s translation. The white colt image comes from the Zhuangzi.
